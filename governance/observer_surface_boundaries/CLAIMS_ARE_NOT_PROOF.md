# CLAIMS ARE NOT PROOF

## Purpose

This note documents an important distinction emerging across governance systems, runtime observability, verification infrastructure, and operational continuity systems:

claims
!=
proof

Many systems preserve meaningful artifacts:

- repositories
- specifications
- receipts
- signatures
- ledgers
- attestations
- telemetry
- dashboards
- reports
- audit trails
- observability layers

But different artifacts preserve different properties.

The existence of an artifact does not automatically prove that a governed boundary held at runtime.

---

# Core Distinction

## Claims

Claims describe or assert something about a system.

Examples include:

- stated policies
- architectural descriptions
- governance declarations
- compliance assertions
- operational guarantees
- enforcement claims
- runtime assumptions
- deployment statements

Claims may accurately describe intended behavior.

But intended behavior is not itself proof of runtime constraint.

---

## Proof

Proof requires independently referable evidence that a boundary actually held under runtime conditions.

Examples may include:

- deterministic replay behavior
- independently reproducible conditions
- bounded execution constraints
- demonstrable interruption viability
- present-state admissibility verification
- independently grounded runtime locality
- verifiable prevention of inadmissible transition formation

Proof concerns boundary behavior.

Not descriptive language alone.

---

# Important Distinction Layers

Different artifacts preserve different failure surfaces.

For example:

A repository may preserve:
- implementation history

A specification may preserve:
- intended architecture

A receipt may preserve:
- recordation

A signature may preserve:
- origin

A ledger may preserve:
- persistence

An attestation may preserve:
- environment

An observability layer may preserve:
- visibility into degradation

A replay system may preserve:
- reproducibility

None of these alone automatically proves:

- consequence-boundary prevention
- runtime admissibility
- interruption viability
- or enforcement validity

---

# Structural Risk

The dangerous surface emerges when:

artifact persistence
inherits
proof status.

Examples include:

- visibility inheriting enforcement legitimacy
- documentation inheriting runtime validity
- coherence inheriting grounding
- continuity inheriting admissibility
- observability inheriting consequence prevention
- specification density inheriting proof authority

At that point systems may appear:

- rigorous
- verified
- governed
- trustworthy
- and operationally grounded

while runtime boundary behavior remains unproven underneath.

---

# Observatory Relevance

The HACR Hybrid Observatory intentionally separates:

- claims
- observability
- reproducibility
- and enforcement

because they do not solve the same failure surfaces.

The observatory itself does not claim:

- sovereign governance authority
- universal enforcement capability
- institutional execution control
- or consequence authorization authority

The purpose is observer-local runtime inspection and degradation visibility under bounded conditions.

The observatory remains:

- observer-local
- bounded
- diagnostic
- reproducibility-oriented
- non-authoritative

Visibility is not enforcement.
Claims are not proof.

# Central Reduction

A system may preserve extensive governance artifacts while still lacking independently demonstrable runtime boundary validity.

Different layers preserve different properties.

Collapsing them into a single “proof” category may itself become a governance failure surface.

UNKNOWN -> HOLD.

Break survivability, not ontology.
