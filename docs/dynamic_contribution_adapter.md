# Dynamic Contribution Adapter

## Purpose

The Dynamic Contribution Adapter is a bounded communication artifact intended to help translate contributions across different technical, operational, governance, audit, and strategic layers without expanding the original claim.

The adapter does not determine truth, authority, legitimacy, compliance, or execution permission.

It supports clearer contribution across domains by preserving:

- original claim scope
- audience context
- operational grounding
- runtime/local constraints
- explicit non-claims
- bounded interpretation

---

## Core Problem

Different audiences often use different language for similar pressure surfaces.

A single concept may be interpreted differently by:

- engineers
- auditors
- operators
- executives
- regulators
- researchers
- governance teams
- infrastructure teams
- reliability teams
- product/growth teams

The risk is that translation can silently become claim expansion.

The adapter exists to prevent that.

---

## Core Principle

Same pressure surface.

Different interpretive language.

No hidden authority inheritance.

---

## Contribution Layers

| Layer | Primary Concern | Safe Contribution Mode |
|---|---|---|
| Runtime / Engineering | What happens in execution | operational example, observable surface |
| Infrastructure / OT | What continues under physical/system constraints | dependency, lifecycle, propagation framing |
| Audit / Review | What can be checked | evidence, reproducibility, limitation framing |
| Governance | What responsibility surface is implicated | boundary, accountability, non-claim framing |
| Executive / Board | What decision risk exists | concise risk, preparedness, exposure framing |
| Research / Philosophy | What concept is being explored | clearly marked conceptual interpretation |
| Product / Growth | What behavior or adoption pattern exists | communication, trust, distribution framing |
| Reliability / Safety | What fails under pressure | containment, rollback, resilience framing |

---

## Adapter Flow

1. Identify the source claim.
2. Identify the target audience.
3. Identify the operational layer.
4. Preserve original scope.
5. Remove excess abstraction.
6. Translate into audience-appropriate language.
7. Add non-claims where needed.
8. Avoid authority inflation.
9. Keep the contribution inspectable.
10. Exit without expanding the system.

---

## Translation Modes

### Engineering Mode

Use when speaking to builders, infrastructure teams, or runtime operators.

Prefer:

- execution path
- dependency surface
- queue
- retry
- replay
- stale state
- runtime visibility
- instrumentation
- observable behavior

Avoid:

- legitimacy claims
- universal governance framing
- abstract authority language

---

### Audit / Reviewer Mode

Use when speaking to auditors, reviewers, or independent assessors.

Prefer:

- reproducibility
- evidence
- receipts
- scope
- limitations
- assumptions
- deterministic output
- review path

Avoid:

- certification claims
- compliance guarantees
- proof-of-safety language

---

### Executive Mode

Use when speaking to leadership or boards.

Prefer:

- exposure
- decision risk
- preparedness
- lifecycle impact
- operational blind spot
- cost of delay
- accountability surface

Avoid:

- dense topology language
- technical overcompression
- speculative doctrine

---

### Governance Mode

Use when speaking in policy, governance, or accountability contexts.

Prefer:

- boundary clarity
- responsibility surface
- oversight limitation
- control visibility
- human review constraints
- operational dependency

Avoid:

- claiming authority
- replacing governance systems
- asserting legitimacy determinations

---

### Research / Conceptual Mode

Use when exploring deeper structural ideas.

Prefer:

- clearly marked hypothesis
- conceptual pressure test
- scoped interpretation
- bounded terminology
- explicit uncertainty

Avoid:

- presenting concepts as operational fact
- ontology expansion without runtime anchor

---

### Human / Relational Mode

Use when the contribution is personal, reflective, spiritual, or emotional.

Prefer:

- sincerity
- respect
- humility
- care
- lived experience
- non-systematizing language

Avoid:

- forcing systems language onto personal experience
- diagnosing or reframing someone’s meaning without invitation

---

## Drift Risks

The adapter should detect and reduce:

- word salad
- semantic overcompression
- ontology recursion
- shared vocabulary becoming substitute authority
- translation becoming claim expansion
- visibility becoming permission
- evidence becoming authority
- conceptual coherence becoming operational validity
- governance language becoming enforcement implication

---

## Boundary Rules

The adapter must not:

- authorize execution
- certify claims
- govern systems
- replace domain expertise
- infer legality
- infer compliance
- create operational authority
- collapse all domains into one vocabulary
- universalize a local pressure surface

---

## Healthy Contribution Pattern

A healthy contribution should usually follow this structure:

1. Acknowledge the useful surface.
2. Translate it into the target layer.
3. Preserve boundaries.
4. Add operational grounding.
5. Avoid overclaiming.
6. Leave room for domain-specific expertise.

Example:

“This seems most useful when treated as a runtime visibility problem rather than a governance conclusion. The concrete surface appears to be delayed invalidation across queues, retries, or downstream dependencies. That makes it inspectable without requiring the translation layer to inherit authority.”

---

## Final Constraint

The Dynamic Contribution Adapter is a communication support artifact.

It helps translate contributions across layers.

It does not decide which layer is correct.

It does not convert shared language into authority.

It does not turn interoperability into governance.

It remains:

- observer-side
- bounded
- non-authoritative
- operationally grounded
- topology-aware
- communication-focused
- revisable
